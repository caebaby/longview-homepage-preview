Work-Optional Is Not Retirement: What It Actually Takes for Physicians to Stop on Their Own Terms

The goal isn’t to stop practicing medicine. It’s to choose whether you do. Here’s what that actually requires financially — and why most physicians this close to the finish line still don’t have a clear plan.

LV
LongView Planning Partners
May 2026  ·  9 min read
Educational content — not investment advice

Most physicians do not actually want to retire. That is not a provocation — it is what the research shows and what pre-retirement physicians say when asked directly. Medicine is identity. It is purpose. It is the thing they trained for, sacrificed for, and in many cases defined themselves by for twenty-plus years. The goal is not to walk away from it.

The goal is to stop being owned by it.

Work-optional is a different destination than retirement, and it requires a different kind of financial planning to reach. This article is not investment advice — it is an educational framework for physicians in the final decade or so before they want to have real options. Every physician’s situation is different. Work with a qualified fiduciary advisor who knows physician careers before making changes to your financial strategy.

What Work-Optional Actually Means

Work-optional means that you practice medicine — or you do not — because you chose to, not because the income is required. The calendar is yours. You can take three months off. You can cut to two days a week. You can leave a hospital system you hate without a financial plan B holding you hostage. You can retire at sixty-two or you can work until seventy-five. The difference is that either path is genuinely available to you.

Physicians who reach this point describe a fundamental shift in how they experience their work. The difficult days are still difficult. The administrative burden is still there. But the emotional register is different when the patient in room four is there because you chose to be there, not because the mortgage requires it.

“It’s amazing how much you can love medicine when you can retire whenever the fancy hits you.”
— David, Physician on FIRE  [VERIFY — confirm attribution and permission before publishing]

This is the target. Not the finish line of retirement — the freedom of optionality. And for physicians twenty or more years into their careers, it is closer than most of them think. It also requires more precise planning than most of them have done.

The 4 Numbers Every Physician Needs Before They Can Claim Work-Optional

Work-optional is not a feeling. It is a mathematical condition. A physician who cannot articulate these four numbers is not yet there, regardless of how much they have accumulated:

  1. Your retirement number — precisely calculated. Not “25 times expenses” applied generically. Your number accounts for your actual spending, your real obligations, the lifestyle you have built, and the years between your target date and Medicare and Social Security eligibility. This number should be calculated by someone who can model it, not estimated on a napkin.
  2. Your Social Security optimization strategy. For physicians who delayed career start due to training, the calculus on when to claim Social Security is different and frequently mishandled. The gap between an optimized claiming strategy and a default one can represent meaningful dollars over a retirement that may span twenty-five to thirty years. This is not a set-it-and-forget-it decision. It should be modeled against your specific break-even points and life expectancy assumptions.
  3. Your distribution strategy — in writing, in sequence. You have spent a career accumulating assets in tax-deferred accounts, taxable accounts, and (if you were advised well along the way) Roth accounts. The order in which you draw down those accounts, the pace at which you convert assets, and the brackets you occupy each year in retirement determine the total taxes you pay over your remaining lifetime. A physician with $3.5M in assets who draws them down randomly pays more in taxes than an equivalent physician with a sequenced distribution plan. This is not a minor difference.   4. Your practice transition value and timeline. For physicians in private practice, partnership, or any setting where the practice itself has equity value, there is a fourth number that often goes unaddressed until it is too late to optimize: what the practice is worth, what the transition structure will be, and what the tax implications of that transaction are. This number is not separate from the retirement plan — it is one of the largest single inputs to it.

Why the Decade Before Retirement Is the Highest-Leverage Tax Planning Window

Most physicians focus their tax planning on the years when income is highest. That instinct is understandable but incomplete. The decade before retirement — specifically, the years between peak income and the point when required minimum distributions begin — is often the most important tax planning window of a physician’s financial life.

The reason is Roth conversion. During the years when earned income drops but before Social Security, RMDs, and other income sources kick in, many physicians find themselves in a bracket window they have not occupied since residency. The assets sitting in their pre-tax retirement accounts will eventually be taxed — at whatever bracket applies when they are distributed. Converting portions of those accounts to Roth during low-bracket years — intentionally, in a calibrated sequence — can reduce lifetime tax burden substantially.

There is also the IRMAA consideration. Medicare premium surcharges apply at income thresholds that many physicians cross without realizing it, and without planning for it. A Roth conversion strategy that takes IRMAA brackets into account looks different than one that does not. Getting this wrong is not a minor clerical error — it is a recurring annual cost that compounds over decades of Medicare eligibility.

Income smoothing — intentionally managing the level and timing of income in the years immediately before and after retirement — is a related lever that most physicians never pull because they do not have the plan in place to pull it. This requires a relationship with a fiduciary advisor who is doing active planning, not just asset management.

Common Mistakes Physicians Make in the Final Decade

These are the patterns that appear most consistently among physicians who are close to work-optional but not yet there:

  • Waiting too long on Roth conversions. Every year that passes inside a high-bracket earning window is a year of conversion opportunity foregone. The optimal conversion window is finite, and many physicians do not start until it has already partially closed.
  • No practice exit plan. A physician who owns equity in a practice and does not have a written, structured exit plan in place five or more years before their target date is leaving money on the table and creating avoidable risk. Practice valuations are affected by factors that can be influenced if approached with lead time — and almost certainly cannot be influenced at the last minute.
  • Underestimating healthcare costs before Medicare. A physician who targets work-optional at age sixty or sixty-two has a two-to-five year gap before Medicare eligibility. Individual market healthcare premiums for a physician at that income and age are not trivial. This is a planning variable that is routinely overlooked until it becomes a shock.
  • An advisor relationship built for accumulation, not distribution. Many physicians have an advisor relationship that was appropriate for the decades of earning and saving. That relationship is not automatically appropriate for the transition to distribution. The math changes. The priorities change. The advisor’s competencies should match what the next phase actually requires.

The Identity Piece No One Plans For

There is a dimension to the work-optional transition that financial plans almost never address: who are you when you are no longer “Dr. [Name]” six days a week?

This is not a soft question. Physicians who have built their identity, their social relationships, and their sense of purpose around the practice of medicine face a real transition challenge when that anchor shifts — even voluntarily, even joyfully. The physicians who plan for this dimension tend to have better outcomes, both financially and personally. They make better decisions about when to step back. They maintain the relationships and structures that keep them engaged with the world. They avoid the common pattern of leaving medicine abruptly, struggling with the loss of identity, and then returning to full-time practice simply because nothing else was in place.

A financial plan for work-optional should include some honest accounting of what the non-financial structure of that life looks like. Not because a financial advisor is a therapist — they are not — but because the financial plan that assumes a physician will happily fill their time with golf and travel may not survive contact with the reality of who that physician actually is.

Practice Exit: What Physicians Should Know 5+ Years Out

For physicians with ownership stakes in a practice, the exit transaction is often one of the most significant financial events of their life. It is also the one they are least likely to have planned for systematically.

Practice valuations are a function of revenue, EBITDA, specialty, payer mix, geographic market, patient panel characteristics, and buyer type — among other factors. Physicians who begin the exit planning process five or more years before their target date can influence several of these variables. Physicians who begin six months before they want to leave cannot.

The tax structure of a practice sale — asset sale versus equity sale, earnout structures, installment arrangements — has a significant impact on what the physician actually keeps. This is not a detail to work out at closing. It is a planning variable that should be addressed years in advance, in coordination with a CPA who has experience with medical practice transactions and a financial advisor who understands how the proceeds integrate into the overall retirement plan.

What a Retirement Readiness Conversation Actually Covers

For physicians approaching the decade before work-optional, the right starting point is a structured readiness conversation — not an asset review, not a portfolio presentation, but a conversation that addresses all four numbers, the tax planning window, the practice exit plan, and the identity question in one frame.

Most physicians twenty or more years into their careers have never had this conversation in its complete form. They have had pieces of it, in separate conversations, with separate people who were not coordinating with each other. The result is a plan that is less than the sum of its parts — and a physician who is closer to work-optional than they realize, but does not yet know it.
