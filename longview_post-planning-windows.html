6 Planning Windows Physicians Often Explore Between Peak Earnings and RMD Age

How age-based planning considerations between 55 and 73 fit into a broader retirement picture.

LV
LongView Planning Partners
May 2026  ·  10 min read
Educational content — not investment advice
In the next 10 minutes, you’ll explore:
  • Six planning topics that involve specific ages or age-based rules under current law between 55 and 73 — and what each one involves
  • Why these topics often come into sharper focus during the years approaching retirement
  • A reflection frame for thinking through where each topic sits in your current planning
KEY TAKEAWAY — THE BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT

Six planning topics involve specific age-based rules under current law between 55 and 73. Each interacts with broader retirement-decade planning in ways that vary household to household.

The potential long-term relevance of looking at these topics together varies significantly by household and may range from minimal to substantial depending on circumstances. In some cases, mapping them in a written, integrated view during this period is useful. In other households, existing arrangements already address them well and no significant changes are pursued. Both outcomes are legitimate.

Why Age-Linked Topics Tend to Come Into Focus During This Period

A common observation in financial planning conversations with physicians 55–62 is a general sense that there are planning topics in front of them that they haven’t yet fully mapped. The topics involve specific age-based rules — Required Minimum Distributions at 73, Medicare’s two-year lookback at 63, Social Security claiming between 62 and 70 — that are calendar-based under current law.

Two structural reasons explain why these topics often surface during this period. First, several of them are tied to ages that aren’t yet immediately in front of someone at 45 or 50 but become more proximate during the years approaching retirement. Second, the topics interact with each other in ways that earlier-career topics generally don’t, which is part of why some physicians find a coordinated view useful during this window.

For some physicians, these topics don’t surface all at once. They tend to emerge gradually — during retirement conversations, tax reviews, beneficiary updates, or moments when several planning decisions start intersecting with each other in ways that hadn’t been obvious before. The relevance of each topic, and whether any of them warrant focused attention, varies significantly by household.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

If you’re somewhere between 55 and 62 and have been thinking about planning topics tied to specific ages in the years ahead, that’s a reasonable area to think through. The relevance of any given topic differs by household. Individual circumstances vary significantly, and the relevance of these considerations differs by case.

Topic 1 — The Years Between Peak Earnings and Required Minimum Distributions

Required Minimum Distributions begin at age 73 under current rules. For households with meaningful balances in pre-tax retirement accounts, the period between when peak earnings end and when RMDs begin is a topic some physicians use to review how future withdrawals and tax rules may interact over time.

How long this period is, and what’s involved in thinking through it, depends on individual retirement timing and account structure. The relevance of considering this period ahead of time varies significantly with account size and household income picture. In some cases, multi-year planning during this window is useful. In other situations, existing arrangements already address what’s needed.

Topic 2 — Medicare Income Threshold Awareness

Medicare premium calculations under current rules use modified adjusted gross income from two years prior. Income at age 63 contributes to premium calculations at 65; income at 64 contributes to calculations at 66.

For physicians who will become Medicare-eligible during their late retirement-transition years, awareness of how income decisions in one year may interact with Medicare costs in a later year is one of the variables certain households think through during this period. Whether this awareness translates into specific income planning depends on individual circumstances. The relevance and magnitude differ by household.

Topic 3 — Social Security Claiming

Social Security claiming under current rules opens at age 62 and the latest claiming age is 70. Claiming timing involves longevity assumptions, spousal earnings record, interaction with other retirement income, and how it relates to broader tax planning. The considerations differ household to household.

Some physicians choose to model several claiming scenarios during the years approaching retirement rather than defaulting to a single claiming age. In other households, existing planning already considers the topic and no further exploration is needed. Individual circumstances vary significantly, and the relevance of these considerations differs by case.

Topic 4 — Income Trajectory Awareness Through the RMD Years

For households with meaningful pre-tax retirement balances, the income that will be required from those accounts under RMD rules at 73 may interact with other retirement income — Social Security, investment income, pension income where applicable — in ways that shape the household income picture during the RMD years.

Some physicians use this period to review how those interactions might unfold over time. Depending on household specifics and the size of pre-tax balances, the topic may warrant focused attention. For other households, the existing approach already accounts for it. The relevance and magnitude differ by case.

A QUICK REFLECTION
Three questions worth thinking about at this halfway point: When you think about the four topics above, which are clearly addressed in your current planning, and which would you like to understand more about? Do you feel you have a clear view of how these topics may interact in your specific situation? If you were to think through your next decade of financial planning, what kind of conversation — focused on a single topic, or looking at several together — would fit your preference? There are no right answers. In some cases, the reflection confirms current planning already addresses these topics well. In others, it surfaces an area where a focused look could be useful.

Topic 5 — Estate Considerations Under Current Law

The federal estate tax exemption is established under current law and is scheduled to change at specified future dates unless legislation modifies the schedule. For households whose total estate — including life insurance death benefits, retirement account balances, and other assets — may approach exemption levels under any future scenario, estate considerations are a topic that some physicians review periodically.

Whether a household’s current planning fully accounts for projected estate exposure, including any mitigation structures that may be relevant under current law, depends on the household’s specifics. Certain households address estate considerations once and review periodically; others revisit the topic more actively during the years approaching retirement. Both approaches can be appropriate. The relevance and magnitude differ by household.

Topic 6 — Beneficiary Designations

Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance policies generally take precedence over wills for those specific assets under current rules. Awareness of how current designations align with broader estate intentions is one of the topics certain households review periodically.

Some physicians choose to revisit beneficiary designations during life transitions — career changes, family changes, or simply during the years approaching retirement when other planning topics are becoming more relevant. In other households, designations are already aligned with current circumstances and no review is needed.

How These Topics Often Relate to Each Other

Some physicians prefer to evaluate these topics together because financial decisions can relate to one another over time. A consideration in one area may have implications for how another is approached. A decision about pre-RMD income planning interacts with Medicare income threshold awareness, which interacts with Social Security claiming timing, which interacts with the broader retirement income picture.

For households where multi-topic coordination during this period adds value, the rationale is typically structural. For other households, the topics are addressed effectively through separate channels — and a coordinated look doesn’t add meaningful value. Where coordination becomes meaningful is largely a function of household specifics, account size, income picture, and household preferences for how integrated the view should be.

Reflection: If you’ve found yourself starting to think about one of these topics on your own and noticing it connecting to several others you hadn’t yet examined — that’s a common observation. It’s one of the reasons some physicians during this period choose to look at the topics together rather than separately.

A Note on Doing This Independently

Certain households explore these topics independently — through their own research, conversations with tax professionals, or periodic consultations with other professionals. Depending on the structure already in place, that exploration may answer the questions a household has. For others, the topics touch enough adjacent considerations that a coordinated look becomes useful during this period.

Whether to pursue these topics independently or in a focused conversation is a household-by-household decision. In some cases, the independent path works well for the situation. In others, looking at the picture with someone who regularly works with medical professionals fits the preference for an integrated view. Both paths are reasonable.

A Reflection Frame: Six Questions to Sit With

Before any conversation, here are six questions that surface where you currently stand on each topic. The presence or absence of clear answers in your own mind is itself useful information — not as a measure of your planning, but as a reflection of which topics you’d like to think through further.

The pre-RMD years. Do you have a written view of how your pre-tax retirement balances will be drawn down across the years between peak earnings and RMDs at 73?

Medicare income thresholds. Do you have a sense of what your modified adjusted gross income may look like at age 63 and 64 — and how that relates to your Medicare cost picture beginning at 65?

Social Security claiming. Have you looked at several claiming scenarios for your household, or do you have a preferred approach in mind that you’ve thought through?

Income trajectory. Do you have a view of how your household income picture may evolve year by year from now through age 80 — including how RMDs at 73 may affect that picture?

Estate considerations. Do you have a current sense of your projected gross estate (including life insurance) relative to current estate exemption levels and any future scheduled changes?

Beneficiary designations. When you think about the beneficiary designations across your retirement accounts and life insurance policies, do they reflect your current household circumstances as you understand them?

If most of these prompt a clear answer, your planning likely addresses these topics well. If several prompt "I’d like to think about this further," that’s not a problem to solve in one step — it’s a useful prompt to consider whether a focused look at the topics together would be useful in your situation.

KEY TAKEAWAY — WHAT TO REMEMBER

Six planning topics involve specific age-based rules between 55 and 73: the pre-RMD years, Medicare income thresholds, Social Security claiming, income trajectory through the RMD years, estate considerations under current law, and beneficiary designations. The relevance and magnitude of each varies significantly by household.

In some cases, looking at these topics together in writing during this period is useful. In others, existing arrangements already address them well. Both outcomes are legitimate.

A useful next step, for households exploring the question, is a conversation that maps which topics are already well-addressed in current planning and which might benefit from a closer look — with no expectation that any changes need to follow.
