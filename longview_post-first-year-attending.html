Your First Year as an Attending: The 5 Financial Decisions That Will Cost You the Most

Your income tripled. The financial mistakes available to you did too. Here’s what most new attendings get wrong in year one — and how to get ahead of it.

LV
LongView Planning Partners
May 2026  ·  8 min read
Educational content — not investment advice

The jump from resident to attending physician is the most financially consequential transition in a physician’s career. In a matter of months, income increases by two, three, or four times. Student loan repayment decisions that seemed abstract suddenly have massive implications. Tax bracket realities hit for the first time. And the financial services industry notices.

The planning gap that opens in year one is not usually about lack of intelligence or ambition. Physicians who spend 7–10 years in training become world-class diagnosticians under extraordinary time pressure. They do not, in the same period, become sophisticated personal finance decision-makers — and there is nothing wrong with that. The problem is that the decisions made in the first 12–18 months of attending practice tend to persist for years, and some cannot be undone.

This article covers the five financial mistakes that most commonly hurt new attendings, what those mistakes cost, and what to do instead. It is for educational purposes only and not a substitute for advice from a qualified financial advisor who understands physician income and career trajectories.

The Five Mistakes

Mistake 01
Lifestyle inflation before savings rate is locked in
What it costs: Compound time — the one resource that never comes back
The attending income jump is real and it is earned. After a decade of training on a resident salary, spending more is both rational and understandable. The problem is sequence. Most physicians who lock in a higher lifestyle first and “plan to start saving later” find that the savings rate never materializes at the level they intended. The spending adjusts. The recurring expenses grow to fill the income. And years later, the physician who intended to be financially ahead realizes they are not significantly further along than a peer who spent freely but happened to maximize their 401(k) automatically.
What to do instead
Set your savings rate first, before the lifestyle expands to fill the income. Even an imperfect rate — say, 20% of gross income directed to retirement accounts and debt payoff — established in month one creates a structural floor that compound interest works on for decades. The lifestyle that builds on top of that floor is still dramatically better than anything residency offered.
Mistake 02
Staying on the wrong loan repayment plan after income changes
What it costs: Potentially tens of thousands of dollars — or a forfeited PSLF path
The income-driven repayment strategy that made sense in residency — low payments, PSLF counting toward forgiveness at a nonprofit employer — requires active reevaluation when attending income arrives. Monthly payment calculations under IBR, PAYE, and other IDR plans are based on income. When income doubles or triples, so do the payment calculations. Physicians who stay on PSLF-track plans with high attending incomes without recalculating often discover they are paying more than they would under aggressive private refinancing — with no benefit offset. Conversely, physicians who refinance immediately without modeling the PSLF math first can permanently forfeit forgiveness that would have been worth more. This decision is not reversible.
What to do instead
Before your first attending paycheck arrives, model both paths with your actual loan balance, your actual income, and your actual employer’s PSLF eligibility status. This is a single analysis that can save significant money. Do it with a qualified professional, not a loan servicer representative whose incentives may not align with your outcome. Mistake 03
Accepting the first advisor who calls
What it costs: Products not aligned with your situation, inappropriate recommendations, misaligned incentives for years
New attendings receive financial advisor outreach from multiple directions — the hospital’s “preferred” advisor, colleagues’ referrals, advisors who specialize in “physician planning.” The branding of physician specialization has become common enough that it no longer signals anything meaningful about whether an advisor is structured in a way that aligns their incentives with yours. Ask any advisor how they’re compensated and whether that changes based on what you buy. That question alone will tell you more than their credential list.
What to do instead
Look for a fiduciary. “Fiduciary” means the advisor is legally required to act in your interest. Ask how they’re compensated and whether their compensation changes based on what you buy — a fee that stays the same regardless of the product removes most of the conflicts of interest from the relationship. That single question tells you more than any credential.
“Filing is not planning. The tax return is the receipt for decisions made months ago — and most new attendings have never had anyone help them make those decisions.”

Mistake 04
Not maxing tax-advantaged accounts in the right order
What it costs: Unnecessary tax drag for years, often five figures annually at physician income levels
Attending physician income almost always places physicians in the top federal tax brackets. Tax-advantaged accounts — 401(k), backdoor Roth IRA, HSA, 457(b) if available — are the most powerful legal tools for reducing that tax burden. The mistake is not always failing to use them entirely, but failing to use them in the right order, or leaving accounts open and unused while investing in taxable brokerage accounts instead. A physician who invests $1,000/month in a taxable brokerage account instead of a tax-advantaged account is paying income tax on contributions and capital gains tax on growth, every year, for their entire career. The cumulative difference over 25–30 years is significant.

Mistake 05
Buying too much house too fast
What it costs: Illiquidity, equity risk, and constraint on future practice flexibility
Physician mortgage loans — products designed specifically for physicians who have high income but student loan debt and limited savings history — allow purchasing a home with little or no down payment and without private mortgage insurance (PMI). This is a genuinely useful product that eliminates a real barrier for physicians early in their careers. The risk is what happens when the physician buys at the upper limit of what the loan product will allow. A home purchased with no down payment has no equity buffer. If the physician needs to relocate — to join a different practice, to take an academic position, to escape a partnership that went wrong — selling a home purchased 18 months ago with no down payment in a flat or declining market means arriving at closing with a check rather than leaving with one. Early career physicians move more than they expect to.
What to do instead
Physician loans are a tool, not a signal to maximize purchase price. Consider buying below what the loan product allows and building equity before upgrading. Or rent for 12–24 months in a new city before purchasing, preserving optionality while learning the local market and confirming the practice environment is a long-term fit. A home is a major financial commitment and a lifestyle commitment simultaneously — both deserve deliberate evaluation.

The Pattern Behind the Mistakes

These five mistakes share a structure: they happen in a narrow window, they feel reasonable at the time, and their consequences compound for years before they become visible. The physician who makes all five in year one is not making bad decisions by any obvious measure — they are spending what they earned, using financial products available to them, and trying to catch up on a lifestyle they delayed through training. The cost is not always apparent until year seven or ten or fifteen, when the financial picture is less advanced than it should be given the income earned.

The physicians who get year one right tend to have something in common: they talked to a fiduciary before making the major decisions. Not after the loan was refinanced, not after the house was purchased, not after the 401(k) sat unfunded for three years — before.
