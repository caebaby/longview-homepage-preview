Tax Planning vs. Tax Filing: Why High-Income Physicians Overpay Every Year

Filing your taxes and planning your taxes are not the same thing. Most physicians who work with a CPA are only getting one of them. Here’s the difference — and what it costs.

LV
LongView Planning Partners
May 2026  ·  8 min read
Educational content — not tax advice

Filing is not planning. Those four words represent the most expensive mistake high-income physicians make every year — and almost no one tells them it’s happening.

Most physicians have a CPA. They hand over their W-2s and 1099s in February or March, get a return prepared, sign it, and move on. They call this “taking care of taxes.” What they’re actually doing is documenting what already happened. The decisions that could have reduced their tax bill — sometimes by tens of thousands of dollars — had to be made months earlier. By April, it’s too late.

This is not a criticism of CPAs. It is a description of how most CPA relationships are structured. Filing a return is a service. Tax planning is a different service. Most physicians are paying for the first one and never receiving the second.

The Distinction That Changes Everything

Tax filing is the process of accurately reporting to the IRS what happened during the prior calendar year. Your CPA collects your income documents, applies current law, and produces a return. Done well, it ensures accuracy and legal compliance. It does not reduce your taxes — it reports them.

Tax planning is the process of making decisions throughout the year — and in prior years — that legally reduce the taxes you will owe. It involves strategy, timing, and proactive coordination between your financial life and your tax situation. It happens in May and October and December, not in March.

The difference between these two things, for a physician earning $400,000 to $600,000 per year, can easily be $15,000 to $40,000 in annual tax liability — legally, using strategies that are available to every physician right now.

The 5 Tax Levers Physicians Have That Most CPAs Never Touch

These are not loopholes or gray areas. They are legitimate, widely-used strategies that require proactive coordination to execute. Most filing-focused CPAs don’t surface them because they don’t have the bandwidth — and because their engagement ends when the return is filed.

  1. Income timing. Physicians with some control over when they receive income — independent contractors, practice owners, or those with bonus structures — may be able to accelerate or defer income between tax years to optimize their bracket exposure. This is a December conversation, not a March one.
  2. Entity structure for practice owners. The question of how your practice is organized — sole proprietor, single-member LLC, S-corporation, or other structure — has significant tax implications at different income levels. An S-corp, for example, can reduce self-employment tax exposure in ways that a sole proprietorship cannot. The right structure depends on your specific income level, practice type, and state. This is a planning conversation, not a filing conversation.
  3. Backdoor Roth IRA and mega backdoor Roth. Most attending physicians earn too much to contribute directly to a Roth IRA. The backdoor Roth strategy — contributing to a traditional IRA and converting it — is a legal workaround that most high-income physicians should be using every year. The mega backdoor Roth, available through certain employer plans, allows after-tax contributions that can be converted to Roth status. Both require proactive coordination between your financial plan and your tax situation. Neither happens automatically.
  4. Tax-loss harvesting coordination. In taxable investment accounts, strategically selling positions at a loss to offset realized gains can reduce your annual tax liability. This is a year-round activity that requires your financial advisor and tax advisor to be coordinating — or for the same team to be doing both.
  5. Charitable giving strategy. Physicians who give to charity may be able to give more tax-efficiently through donor-advised funds, qualified charitable distributions from IRAs, or bunching donations into alternating years to exceed the standard deduction. These strategies only work if they’re planned in advance.

Why Most Physician CPAs Are Filing-Focused

This is a structural issue, not an individual failure. CPA firms that serve high-income individuals get paid per return. A physician who needs a complex return filed is worth $1,500 to $4,000 to a typical CPA firm. A physician who needs year-round tax planning is a different kind of relationship — one that requires more time, more meetings, and ongoing coordination with the physician’s financial advisor.

“Filing is documentation. Planning is strategy. Most physicians are paying for documentation and wondering why their tax bill keeps growing.”
— LongView Planning Partners

Most CPA firms are not set up to provide the second kind of relationship. The economics don’t work for them at typical engagement rates. The result is that a physician who genuinely believes they are “working with a CPA on taxes” may be receiving only filing services — which is necessary but not sufficient.

The physician’s financial planner, if they have one, is often not coordinating with the CPA at all. Each professional does their job without awareness of what the other is doing. The physician’s tax situation is never optimized as a whole.

What Year-Round Tax Planning Actually Looks Like

For physicians working with advisors who do this well, tax planning is not a once-a-year event. It includes a mid-year projection in June or July — when there’s still time to act — and a year-end planning conversation in November or December to execute any remaining strategies before the calendar closes. It means your advisor knows your income situation well enough to flag opportunities as they arise, not after the fact.

It also means someone is looking at your full financial picture — your investments, your retirement accounts, your practice structure if applicable, and your charitable giving — through a tax-aware lens throughout the year. That coordination is the difference between filing and planning.

The Roth Opportunity Most Physicians Miss

The backdoor Roth IRA is perhaps the single most consistently underutilized strategy for high-income physicians. Because most physicians earn above the income limits for direct Roth IRA contributions, the direct path is closed to them. But the backdoor strategy — making a non-deductible traditional IRA contribution and then converting it to Roth — remains available regardless of income.

The mega backdoor Roth, available through certain 401(k) plans that allow after-tax contributions and in-plan conversions, can extend this strategy to significantly larger dollar amounts. Both strategies produce tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals in retirement, which is particularly valuable for physicians who expect to remain in high tax brackets for the rest of their careers.

These strategies are not complicated. They require coordination, annual execution, and awareness of the IRS rules around what is called the “pro-rata rule” for backdoor Roth conversions. But they are legal, widely used, and financially meaningful over a career. The physicians who miss them typically do so not because the strategy is unavailable to them, but because no one raised it.

This article is not a substitute for individualized advice. The specifics of any Roth strategy depend on your income, existing account balances, and plan documents. Consult a qualified financial planner before executing.

Practice Owners: The Entity Structure Decision

Physicians who own their practice face a tax planning layer that W-2 physicians do not: the question of how the practice is structured. This decision — between sole proprietorship, single-member LLC, S-corporation, and other structures — affects how practice income is taxed and how much self-employment tax the physician pays.

The S-corporation structure, in particular, allows practice owners to split income between a salary (subject to payroll taxes) and a distribution (not subject to payroll taxes at the same rate). At certain income levels, this structure can produce meaningful annual tax savings. At lower income levels, the administrative overhead of maintaining an S-corp may not be worth it. The crossover point depends on your specific income, state, and practice type.

These decisions need to be made — or at least reviewed — proactively. They cannot be revisited after year-end. And they interact with retirement plan strategy in ways that make coordination between your financial planner and your tax advisor essential.
