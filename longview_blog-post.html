PSLF in 2026: What Attending Physicians Need to Recalculate Right Now

The rules changed. Again. Here’s what it means for attending physicians currently on income-driven repayment.

LV
LongView Planning Partners
May 2026  ·  8 min read
Educational content — not investment advice

If you finished residency or fellowship in the last five to ten years with six-figure federal student loan balances, there is a strong chance you built a repayment plan around PSLF — Public Service Loan Forgiveness. And if you enrolled in SAVE (Saving on a Valuable Education), the income-driven repayment plan that replaced REPAYE, your plan may no longer be what you thought it was.

This article is not legal or tax advice. What it is: a clear-eyed overview of what changed, what questions attending physicians should be asking right now, and what a thoughtful evaluation process looks like. Every physician’s loan situation is different. Physicians should work with a qualified advisor — ideally one with student loan expertise — before making any repayment changes.

What Changed with PSLF and SAVE in 2025–2026

SAVE, the income-driven repayment plan introduced in 2023, was immediately popular among borrowers with high debt relative to income — a category that describes a significant portion of attending physicians early in their careers. The plan featured lower monthly payments and a provision that prevented interest from capitalizing when payments didn’t fully cover monthly interest.

Beginning in mid-2024, SAVE became the subject of federal legal challenges. Courts placed the plan in administrative limbo, and millions of borrowers who had enrolled in SAVE — or were in the process of enrolling — found their accounts in a period of extended forbearance. As of 2026, the status of SAVE remains unresolved at the federal level, with ongoing litigation determining whether the plan survives in its current form, is modified, or is eliminated.

For PSLF purposes, the critical question is how this period of forbearance is treated toward the 120 qualifying payment count. Physicians working at nonprofit hospitals, academic medical centers, or government health systems who were counting on SAVE payments to accumulate PSLF credits need to verify — not assume — how their servicer is counting this period. The rules are not uniform, and guidance continues to evolve.

Separately, there have been legislative and regulatory discussions at the federal level about changes to income-driven repayment caps, interest subsidies, and the PSLF program itself. The landscape is shifting in ways that affect long-term projections significantly.

What Attending Physicians Need to Do Right Now

  1. Log into your servicer account and pull your payment history. Confirm your current payment count, your current IDR plan, and what plan you’re being counted on. If your account shows a forbearance period, document the dates and contact your servicer to confirm whether that period will count toward PSLF. Get confirmation in writing where possible.
  2. Verify your employer eligibility. Not all nonprofit employment qualifies for PSLF. Your hospital or health system may be a 501(c)(3) entity — or it may not. Submit an updated Employment Certification Form (ECF) for your current employer and confirm your EIN is on the approved list. Physicians who have changed employers since training should re-verify each employer.
  3. Model the refinancing alternative with current numbers. Refinancing out of federal loans into private loans permanently forfeits PSLF eligibility. This is an irreversible decision. That said, if the PSLF path has been materially disrupted, refinancing to a lower interest rate may represent a better financial outcome depending on your balance, income trajectory, and practice setting. Physicians should model both scenarios with current projected income, not income from five years ago.
  4. Evaluate your IDR plan options. If SAVE is no longer viable, the alternative IDR plans — IBR, PAYE, and ICR — have different monthly payment calculations, different interest capitalization rules, and different forgiveness timelines. Physicians may want to evaluate which plan produces the best outcome given the current legal environment and their personal situation.
  5. Get a Certified Student Loan Professional (CSLP®) review before making any changes. The consequences of a wrong move on a $250,000–$400,000 loan balance are significant. A one-time professional review by an advisor with student loan expertise is inexpensive relative to the cost of a misstep.

The Decision Tree: Should You Stay on IDR or Refinance?

There is no universal answer to whether an attending physician should stay the PSLF course or refinance into private loans. The decision depends on several variables that are specific to each person.

Physicians who may want to continue evaluating PSLF typically work at qualifying nonprofit or government employers, have balances that are large relative to their projected income over the next few years, and have already accumulated a meaningful number of qualifying payment counts that would be forfeited by refinancing.

Physicians who may want to seriously model the refinancing alternative typically practice in for-profit settings, have incomes that have grown significantly beyond what the IDR formula assumes, or have smaller balances where the math of aggressive private refinancing may come out ahead — especially if interest rates are favorable at the time of application.

“For physicians carrying $300k+ in loans, this single decision is often worth more than any investment we’ll ever make for them.”

The framing we use with physicians: think of the loan decision as a financial planning question, not a math-only question. PSLF has administrative risk — rules change, servicers make errors, and political environments shift. Refinancing has interest rate risk and the permanent loss of federal protections. Neither path is without tradeoffs. The goal is to make the choice with full information, not partial information.

Physicians may also want to evaluate the timing of any decision carefully. If your PSLF payment count is low (under 40–50 payments), the financial case for staying the PSLF course is harder to make in an uncertain regulatory environment. If your count is high (80+ payments), the value of not starting over is very large, and changes to the program may have less impact on your remaining timeline.

The Bottom Line

The disruption to SAVE and ongoing uncertainty around income-driven repayment plans has created a real, time-sensitive need for attending physicians to review their student loan strategy — not the strategy they set up in residency, but the strategy that makes sense given today’s rules, today’s income, and today’s loan balances.

The physicians most at risk are those who set up SAVE or another IDR plan, enrolled in PSLF tracking, and then moved on and stopped actively monitoring. A lot has changed. Physicians should consider scheduling time specifically to review their current situation, preferably with an advisor who understands both the federal loan system and the income trajectory of a physician career.
